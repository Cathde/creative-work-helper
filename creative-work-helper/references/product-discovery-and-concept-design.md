# Product Discovery and Concept Design

Use this reference for an early digital or physical product, service, feature, or interaction concept when the problem, solution, or evidence is still uncertain. Move between understanding, framing, exploring, and testing as evidence changes; do not treat the process as a mandatory linear checklist.

## Scope and Boundary

Help the user discover the right problem, develop distinct solution mechanisms, and reduce the most consequential uncertainty before expensive delivery. Stop at an evidence-informed concept and validation plan.

Do not silently expand into pixel-level UI, design-system production, a complete PRD, engineering architecture, backlog or roadmap management, or launch execution. When the user requests those outcomes, finish the relevant concept decisions, state what remains unresolved, and prepare a handoff brief for the appropriate next workflow.

## 1. Understand the Situation and Evidence

Clarify only what changes the product direction:

- target user, context, trigger, and desired progress
- current behavior, workaround, substitute, or competing solution
- observed friction, unmet need, or opportunity
- available evidence and its source
- organizational goal, constraints, stakeholders, and decision owner

Separate **Known**, **Tentative**, and **Open**. Treat interviews, observation, behavioral data, support evidence, and credible research as evidence in proportion to their quality. Do not rename stakeholder preference, market convention, or an untested belief as a user insight. When evidence is missing, state the hypothesis and propose the cheapest useful research or test.

## 2. Frame the Opportunity

Express the opportunity without embedding a preferred feature:

```text
For [user in context], [current situation or barrier] makes it hard to [desired progress].
We want to improve [observable outcome], while respecting [critical constraint].
```

Define success at the level appropriate to the stage: learning, behavior, user outcome, business outcome, or delivery readiness. Distinguish an outcome from a shipped feature. Reframe when the original brief assumes a solution or contradicts the evidence.

## 3. Explore Product Mechanisms

Map several concepts that create value differently rather than listing feature variations. Change axes such as user role, trigger, value exchange, interaction loop, automation level, social model, channel, service component, or degree of user control.

For each concept, show:

- value proposition and target situation
- core mechanism or interaction loop
- key user scenario or journey
- what it replaces or improves
- enabling capabilities and explicit non-goals
- main evidence gap and tradeoff

Avoid combining all appealing capabilities into one concept before the underlying mechanism is understood.

## 4. De-risk Before Selecting

Choose three to five criteria that can change the decision and label their priority Tentative. Examine:

- **Value**: whether the target user needs or wants the outcome enough to change behavior.
- **Usability**: whether the user can understand, access, and complete the key journey.
- **Feasibility**: whether technology, operations, data, policy, and capabilities can support it.
- **Business or strategic fit**: whether the model, incentives, risks, and organizational direction support it.

Also consider accessibility, privacy, safety, trust, inclusion, and unintended behavior where relevant. Do not use a universal priority order: the highest-risk uncertainty should lead the next decision.

## 5. Prototype to Learn

Do not prototype the entire imagined product. Select the assumption whose failure would most damage the concept, then define:

```text
Critical assumption
Learning question
Lowest-fidelity artifact or simulation that can answer it
Relevant participants and context
Observable signal
Decision rule: continue, revise, or stop
```

Match fidelity to the learning question. Use a description, storyboard, paper flow, clickable path, concierge simulation, or narrow functional prototype before expensive production when it can produce the needed evidence. Avoid asking only whether people “like” the idea; test comprehension, behavior, willingness, usability, or feasibility directly.

## 6. Prepare the Concept Package

If the user has not specified a deliverable, propose this package for revision:

```text
Problem and evidence
Target user, context, and desired outcome
Confirmed, Tentative, and Open assumptions
Opportunity statement
Considered concepts and selection logic
Selected value proposition and mechanism
Key scenario, journey, or interaction loop
Enabling capabilities and non-goals
Value, usability, feasibility, and strategic risks
Prototype and validation plan
Open decisions and next-workflow handoff
```

The concept is ready to hand off when the user can explain whom it helps, what progress it enables, why this mechanism is preferable, what evidence supports it, and what must be learned next.
